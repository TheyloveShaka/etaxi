Section 4Strategic fit and policy alignment

eTaxi fits the stated direction of national e-mobility policy and fills a gap in the state-backed electric bus programme. It does not depend on any policy that has yet to be confirmed.

4.1 National e-mobility policy and incentives

The National E-Mobility Strategy aims to make Uganda a net source of e-mobility solutions, with local production capacity of half a million vehicles by 2030 at 65% local content, and to electrify public transport such as buses, motorcycles and light rail. Sources disagree on whether it launched in 2023 or 2024. Its incentives include a ten-year income tax exemption for makers of vehicles, batteries and charging equipment, and VAT exemptions for locally made electric vehicles and for charging equipment and services.

The tax position of an imported 14-seat electric minibus is not known. Fully built electric vehicles attract 25% import duty, yet the 2024/25 budget was reported to exempt electric vehicles from VAT, stamp duty and income tax, and one report says an earlier duty waiver was reversed. The reports conflict and Section 14 treats the question as a ruling to obtain from the Uganda Revenue Authority.

4.2 National Development Plan IV and Vision 2040

The transport targets of the fourth National Development Plan (NDP IV), of Vision 2040 and of the National Transport Master Plan were not retrieved in the research for this proposal, so alignment with them is not claimed. Checking each target is a task in the first 90 days (Section 16). The 2010 JICA study recommended a metropolitan transport authority and a shift from paratransit to mass transit; this proposal's operating model is compatible with both.

4.3 Greater Kampala programmes

  • GKMA Urban Development Programme. Parliament cleared a World Bank loan headlined at USD 556 million, mainly for roads, drainage and markets; at least 74.5 km of roads are to be upgraded. Better roads help any vehicle, and the programme is not a transport operator.
  • Light rail. A memorandum with Orascom Construction began an 18-month feasibility study in March 2026 for routes including Kampala to Entebbe, Kira and Wakiso.

4.4 Complementing the state-backed electric buses

E-Bus Xpress runs on trunk corridors such as City Square to Ntinda and from Ntinda to Kira, with a target of at least 300 buses by December 2027. Taxi operators complained that tax-exempt, government-funded buses create an unlevel playing field (a summary of the report). The proposal's view is that the two serve different jobs and should be planned together.

Section 5Proposed solution

An electric 14-seater that looks and works like the taxi Kampala knows, with fixed routes, fares shown first, mobile money and arrival times, charged at depots overnight.

5.2 What is and is not known about price

The price of an electric 14-seater in Uganda is not known. The nearest figures are a conventional Hiace taxi at about UGX 74 million, with tyres and conversion work taking total capital above UGX 80 million, and a global average battery pack price of USD 108 a kilowatt hour in 2025, with LFP packs at USD 81. At the average price an 86.1 kWh pack is worth about USD 9,299 Indicative; that is a component price, not a vehicle price. BasiGo bus operators were reported to acquire vehicles for around USD 7,700 in Kenya and USD 8,800 in Rwanda before paying mileage-based fees, but this is a bus programme. The pilot's tender would produce the first Ugandan price.

5.3 Depot charging

Proposed Vehicles would charge overnight at depots on alternating-current chargers, with a few direct-current fast chargers for a midday top-up at busy terminals. The model puts a base-case vehicle at 43.7 kWh a day, so a pilot of 30 needs about 1,311 kWh a day Indicative. A slow station is reported at about USD 2,500 and a fast station with permits at about USD 35,800.

The Q4 2026 commercial tariff averages UGX 562.1 a kWh (peak 666.5, off-peak 429.7) and the medium industrial tariff averages UGX 363.8; the schedule has no category for electric vehicle charging. Kiira Motors asked in 2023 for a flat UGX 270 a unit at charging points; whether it was adopted was not found.

5.4 The service

Before the trip

Routes start at existing stages. The fare for the whole trip is shown in the app and on the stage board before boarding. The app shows the next vehicle and a live arrival time, and works on low-end Android phones.

On the trip

Payment is by mobile money or card. Riders press Masaawo, the stop-request button, instead of calling out. Onboard cameras and a trip-sharing link let a rider send a live trip to a contact.

Optional extras

Door-to-door pickup is offered at a premium on selected routes. Its price is not set; the pilot would test willingness to pay. A rider can rate the trip and report a fault.

Cashless payment has a local precedent: E-Bus Xpress takes only MTN Mobile Money, Airtel Money and wristbands. Proposed eTaxi would accept cash at staffed stage kiosks during the pilot until usage data shows it can be dropped. Fares would be set with the regulator; current reference points are UGX 1,000 to 2,000 for short taxi trips before the crackdown and UGX 800 to 1,800 on E-Bus Xpress. Masaawo is this proposal's name for the button, taken from the stop call; riders would test the name in the pilot.

Section 6Operating and privatisation model

Kampala's taxi sector is already privately owned, so the aim is structured private delivery under public regulation: franchises, service standards and an operating company the existing owners can join.

6.1 What the term means here

Drivers hire vehicles from private owners for a daily charge, yet the sector has no routes, timetables or fares to enforce. In this proposal, privatisation means three things: the public authority sets routes, fares, safety and data standards; a private operating company delivers the service against those standards; and existing owners can take part as shareholders or lessees rather than be displaced. The only franchise-like precedent found is Pioneer's 2012 route allocation, and no 2026 official statement on franchising taxi routes was found.

6.2 Structure

  • Route or area franchises Proposed: awarded by the regulator for fixed terms, with fare bands, service standards and data-sharing duties written into the contract.
  • An operating company Proposed: not yet registered, owning or leasing vehicles and depots. Existing owners could surrender a condemned taxi for shares, or lease vehicles with an option to own.
  • Pay-as-you-drive leasing Proposed: BasiGo describes a simple mileage-based fee, with no amount published for the Ma3e; an eTaxi fee would bundle energy, maintenance and a share of vehicle cost, and its level is not known.

6.4 A just transition for drivers and conductors

The number of drivers and conductors in the Kampala taxi sector, and their earnings, were not found; the pilot would survey them. Proposed The plan has four parts. First, drivers of surrendered vehicles get priority for training and employment; Kiira Motors has trained about 400 drivers for its buses, which shows the training can be done. Second, conductors, whose cash handling falls with mobile money, are offered roles as stage hosts, charging attendants and safety marshals. Third, driver vetting and refresher training follow the rules set for buses in August 2026, which include one-year badges and biometric log-in; whether they apply to 14-seaters is not stated.
