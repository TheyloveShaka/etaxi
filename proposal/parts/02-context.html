Section 1Executive summary

eTaxi is a concept for electric 14-seater public taxis in Kampala, privately financed and operated under public regulation, with a rider app and a fleet data platform. It is a concept only: nothing is operating, no company is registered and no funding has been raised.

The problem. On 24 August 2026 the Ministry of Works and Transport and the Uganda Police Traffic Directorate began a 100-day crackdown on commuter taxis in dangerous mechanical condition across Greater Kampala. The test is vehicle condition, not age. It still removes supply that commuters depend on, and operators say they cannot afford replacements. No scrappage or fleet-renewal loan scheme was found in the research behind this proposal.

The proposal. A licensed operating company, in which existing taxi owners and associations can hold shares or lease vehicles, would run electric 14-seaters on fixed routes from existing stages, with the fare shown before boarding, payment by mobile money and a stop-request button called Masaawo. A data platform would give riders arrival times and give regulators and planners trip and condition data they do not have today. The state-backed electric buses serve trunk corridors; eTaxis would serve feeder and cross-town routes and give condemned 14-seaters a renewal path.

The numbers. This is an energy cost comparison, not a business plan. In the base case a petrol 14-seater spends UGX 106,900 a day on fuel and an electric one UGX 24,553 on electricity. The saving is worth about UGX 103.9 million over eight years at the average shilling lending rate, and about UGX 152.9 million at the foreign-currency rate. The price of an electric 14-seater in Uganda is not known; the pilot would establish it.

The plan. A pilot of 30 vehicles in months 0 to 12, a corridor rollout of 300 in years 2 to 3 and network scale of 2,000 in years 4 to 6, with a gate between phases. At 2,000 vehicles the model gives about 25,000 tonnes of CO2 avoided a year.

What we ask

Government: a pilot licence and stage access. Operators: co-design and first shareholding. Financiers: pilot and feasibility funding. Technology and research partners: platform, data and evaluation support. Section 16 sets out the asks and a 90-day plan.

Section 2Background: how Kampala got here

Kampala moves mainly on privately owned 14-seater taxis that were never given routes, timetables or fares. Three attempts to replace them with buses have failed or stalled, and a fourth, state-backed, began in May 2026.

2.1 From state buses to private minibuses

In 1972 a private bus company was nationalised to form the Uganda Transport Company, serving mainly Kampala and western Uganda; the report is seen only in summary. That company and the People's Transport Company collapsed in the early 1990s after vandalised and cannibalised parts and fare fraud, which opened the market to private minibus operators. Since then 14-seaters have been licensed with no fixed routes, timetables, fares or formal stops.

2.2 The taxi associations and KCCA

The sector is already private. Drivers hire the minibus from an owner for a daily charge and pay fuel and terminal fees. For nearly 30 years the taxi association UTODA managed taxis and parks under a KCCA contract; the start (about 1986) and end (about 2012) dates could not be confirmed. Reports conflict on how it ended: one says KCCA cancelled the contract in 2011, another that a KCCA email extended its management to 31 October 2014. KCCA then took over the parks and set a monthly fee of UGX 155,000 per taxi in place of daily dues; UTODA had collected over UGX 1 billion a month from about 7,000 taxis.

2.3 The bus attempts

Pioneer Easy Bus began in 2012 under a special regulation, with fares fixed at UGX 500 in the city centre and UGX 1,000 outside. It planned 500 buses, about 100 arrived at first, the bus lanes and terminals the state was to build never appeared, and the service failed within about a year. Tondeka Metro, announced on 12 December 2019 with 980 buses, did not materialise because ownership, financing, operation and regulation were unclear.

2.4 The electric buses of May 2026

E-Bus Xpress, run by Kiira Motors Corporation through a subsidiary, began scheduled cashless electric bus service in Kampala in May 2026 on City Square to Ntinda; the launch date of 4 May comes from a search summary. Fares run from UGX 800 to UGX 1,800 and the government plans 100 electric buses in the Greater Kampala Metropolitan Area (GKMA) by December 2026 and at least 300 by December 2027. The fleet size is reported inconsistently as 21, 23 or 30 buses, and passenger capacity as 42 or up to 90; this proposal does not resolve either conflict. Taxi operators were not consulted before the launch, their vice chairperson said, adding:

"We need to be part of the conversation." Mustapha Mayambala, UTOF vice chairperson (administration), 5 May 2026.

Section 3Problem statement

The fleet is old, renewal is unfinanced, fuel is a volatile cost and commuters have no arrival information. A crackdown that tests vehicle condition has exposed all four at once.

3.1 An ageing fleet

The Daily Monitor reported in March 2023 that most Kampala taxis were up to 30 years old and gave no average. A 2025 remote-sensing study of Kampala found that diesel minibus taxis averaged 25 years old and emitted nitrogen oxides more than nine times the Euro 4 limit. The current number of taxis is not known: 2020 registration counts range from 7,340 to over 15,000 and a trade-press summary suggests 15,000 to 25,000 14-seaters in the GKMA. The pilot would count them.

Operators blame the age on the 2018 ban on importing vehicles 15 years or older, on the cost of newer vans and on exclusion from reforms. In March 2023 the chairman of UTOF said a legally importable used Toyota Hiace costs UGX 80 million to UGX 90 million and a new one carries at least UGX 28 million in taxes. A 2026 bill would lower the import limit from 15 to 13 years with a 50% environmental levy above that age; a minority report argues it would raise prices, and whether it passed is unconfirmed.

3.2 Operation Ganyegenya: what it is

The Ministry of Works and Transport launched a 100-day joint operation with the Uganda Police Traffic Directorate, announced at a Kampala press briefing by Minister Fred Byamukama on 20 August 2026 (articles vary on his title) and started on Monday 24 August 2026. It covers Kampala, Wakiso and Mukono, with checkpoints on Gayaza, Jinja and Hoima Roads.

Be exact: condition, not age

The operation targets taxis in dangerous mechanical condition (DMC), described as "beyond repair". Well-kept newer vans are not targeted. No taxi age cap and no 14-seater ban was found in any 2026 source. The article of 20 August refers to an earlier proposal to phase out 14-seaters in favour of high-capacity buses; no current official plan to do so was found.

Police listed worn tyres, defunct brakes and headlights, and broken or rusty bodies; some vehicles were judged by sight and drivers were sent home, not arrested. Number plates of impounded vehicles were confiscated, and a vehicle returns only after re-inspection and a Certificate of Fitness at the government inspection facilities.

3.3 Numbers so far

On day one police reported 178 vehicles impounded, while another report said more than 400 were impounded and about half retained; the two conflict and the articles do not say why. The 100 days run to about 2 December 2026, the proposal's arithmetic from the start date, as no official end date was found.

A later ministry update reported inspection results for the taxis taken to the government centres at Namanve and Kawanda: by 15 October 2026, 531 had been tested, 423 (79.7 percent) had failed for dangerous mechanical condition and 108 had passed. The listed defects were brakes, steering, tyres, suspension and lights. Police also extended the operation into night hours after grounded taxis were found working after dark. Four in five of the vans that reach a test fail it, which is the clearest sign yet that repair alone will not return the grounded fleet to service.

The run-up is thinly documented. On 18 to 19 March 2026 the Works State Minister issued a 20-day directive with removals to begin on 8 April, and the UTOF chairman said he learned of it from the media. What happened on or after 8 April is not known from the sources used. The minister has said the government will not back down; the same report notes that past crackdowns were often suspended after pressure from operators. The result of the review meeting promised on 26 August is not known.

3.4 The supply shock and fares

A Mukono taxi leader estimated that about 75% of taxis stayed off the road by 26 August; this is his estimate, not a count. Many operators parked vehicles on the advice of UTOF before they could be impounded. Commuters waited more than 40 minutes, and only two taxis were seen heading to Kisaasi from the city centre in one hour. Uganda Railways Corporation capacity is about 4,000 passengers a day and it had added no services by 26 August.

3.5 Fleet renewal has no finance

No scrappage scheme and no fleet-renewal loan was found. Impounded vehicles are held until repaired and certified, not scrapped. The average shilling lending rate was 18.26% in April 2026 against a Central Bank Rate of 9.75% held on 13 August 2026; a commercial vehicle loan rate was not found, so the average rate is used as a proxy.

3.6 Fuel cost exposure

Drivers reported using about 20 litres a day on routes of about 12 km to and from the city and 25 to 30 litres on outskirts routes. Kampala petrol cost UGX 5,090 to UGX 5,400 a litre in April 2026 and UGX 5,140 to UGX 5,599 in a later survey. A highest recorded rate of UGX 6,499 for premium petrol on 15 June 2026 was also reported; it is a maximum, not an average, and the source page was not identified. Pump prices are deregulated, and the 2026 rise was linked to disruption at the Strait of Hormuz.

3.7 Air quality, safety and information

Kampala's 2025 annual average PM2.5 was 44.2 micrograms per cubic metre, tenth among world capitals, against a World Health Organization guideline of 5. Police recorded 26,044 road crashes and 5,383 deaths in 2025; no national breakdown for taxis was found. Riders cannot see when a taxi will arrive. A volunteer-built GTFS feed for Kampala exists, but no real-time feed for paratransit was found.

The proposal's view

The crackdown is a symptom. It removes the worst vehicles but offers no route to better ones, so the likely result is repaired old vans, higher fares and a repeat in the next campaign. An independent commentator made a similar point, calling bad taxis "not the disease; they are the fever".
